Depth-variance guided next-best-view planning

Problem

A drone flying around an object has to decide where to point the camera next. A fixed orbit spends time on surfaces that are already covered and misses whatever the first pass occluded.

Result

70 ± 4 percent surface coverage against 40 ± 3 percent for a tuned orbit, inside a 420 MB GPU budget, with viewpoint scoring costing 2.5 percent of mission time.

My part

First author. Built the Gazebo simulation with a PX4 quadcopter, implemented the acquisition function, and ran the sixty missions the results rest on.

The methodThe reconstruction is 3D Gaussian Splatting, and the renderer carries arbitrary per-Gaussian channels, so depth and depth squared pass through it together. One render then returns the mean depth and the second moment along every ray, and the difference between them is a per-pixel variance: the disagreement among the surfaces the model believes might be there. Accumulated opacity, the signal normally used, stops being informative once the model has placed something at every pixel. Candidate viewpoints come from a 95-point Fibonacci hemisphere, scored on variance and unseen area, then penalised for angular closeness to the viewpoints already chosen that round, so a batch of four spreads around the object.

The setupGazebo Harmonic with a PX4 X500 quadcopter and a 640 × 480 RGB-D gimbal camera, on an RTX 4070 Ti SUPER, with the model capped at 500K Gaussians and trained at 320 × 240. A mission is 4 seed views followed by 11 rounds of 4, for 48 views. Depth variance held about three times the dynamic range of opacity over a mission, while alpha coverage saturated near 0.66 by the third round.

The comparison was run at equal training iterations as well as at convergence. The planner trained on all views each round while the baseline used a sliding window of the ten nearest, so a result reported only at convergence would have credited the viewpoint selection with a gain that came from the training schedule.

D-optimal next-best-view planning for active Gaussian splatting

Problem

Where the round-trip delay is too long for an operator to choose views, the vehicle has to rank candidate viewpoints on its own, before it has seen the images those viewpoints would return.

Result

Over a fifty-view budget the D-optimal policy kept assigning non-zero gain after geometric coverage had already saturated, which is the behaviour a coverage-driven planner cannot produce.

My part

Co-author, fourth of five. Helped develop the planner and its evaluation, alongside the depth-variance work that followed it.

The methodOne hundred thousand Gaussians are initialised on a Poisson mesh obtained from an RGB-D survey. Their positions are frozen and only appearance parameters are learned, which makes the model's information content tractable. Viewpoints are then ranked by D-optimal information gain computed from a diagonal Fisher Hessian accumulated over the current training set. Scoring uses only the candidate pose and the current model, so the image at that pose is not needed in advance.

The failure it exposedAnalysis of the Taylor linearisation underlying the Fisher criterion showed the ranking is reliable only once the model has converged between steps, and that a single seed view leaves an unobserved parameter subspace whose information gain is degenerate. Four azimuthally separated seed views remove that subspace.

The mesh prior is what the depth-variance planner later removes. Freezing positions on a Poisson mesh makes the Fisher information well posed, at the cost of an RGB-D survey before the mission and a geometry the planner cannot revise in flight.

Field estimation and adaptive sampling under positional uncertainty

Problem

Adaptive sampling work generally treats measurement locations as known. An underwater vehicle records each sample against a drifted pose. The value is correct and the coordinate is not.

Result

Drift raised reconstruction error on every field, worst on the fields otherwise easiest to reconstruct, and the estimator hid it: marginal-likelihood fitting absorbed the corruption by raising the noise term instead of correcting the model, so the fault reaches the output disguised as measurement noise.

My part

Sole author, advised by Prof. Jnaneshwar Das. Calibrated the drift model, built the simulation study and ran all 2,000 reconstructions.

The methodThe localisation error was characterised first, from a logged visual-odometry run of 973 keyframes. The drift is an accumulating, temporally correlated process, distinct from independent pose noise, and it grows about two and a half times faster where the camera loses features over open water. A one-parameter random walk calibrated to that run reproduced both the magnitude and the temporal structure. Five synthetic temperature fields were then reconstructed from a budget-limited survey, under exact positions and under injected drift, with two kernels and with both fixed coverage and variance-based adaptive sampling.

What governs itThe additional error concentrated where the field gradient is steep, and it approached a floor that further samples did not breach. At fixed magnitude the temporal correlation of the drift mattered little next to the magnitude itself. The governing quantity is the product of the squared field gradient with the covariance of the positional error.

Variance-based adaptive sampling gained only marginally over a fixed coverage raster. Maximum-variance selection under a stationary kernel behaves much like uniform coverage, and the sampler itself reads the corrupted pose. Weighting samples by logged pose covariance recovered a modest fraction of the lost accuracy, largest on sharp-structured fields.

Low-cost ASV for aquatic field reconstruction

Problem

Mapping a lake's water quality normally means a crewed boat and manual sampling, which is slow, expensive and sparse. The measurements that result are too thin to resolve local structure.

Result

An autonomous vessel costing under US$2,500 reconstructed temperature and water-quality fields from a single survey, and a non-stationary Matérn kernel preserved local peaks that stationary kernels smoothed away, concentrating uncertainty on the structure that was actually undersampled.

My part

Co-first author, equal contribution. Owned the sensing and data path: registering each measurement to the navigation solution, checking timing and completeness across sensors, and deciding which records were admitted to the analysis.

The platformThe R/V Karin Valentine, a low size, weight and power surface vessel built in the DREAMS Lab, running a Pixhawk flight controller on PX4 with an ODROID companion computer for ROS 2. The payload is a 1-D sonar for depth and a multi-probe water-quality sonde on a motorised winch, which descends in one-metre steps and dwells at each depth.

The methodScalar fields were reconstructed from a lawnmower survey by Gaussian process regression, comparing squared-exponential and Matérn kernels against a non-stationary Matérn whose local length-scale matrix varies with position. Hyperparameters were fit by maximising the log marginal likelihood with a Cholesky factorisation of the covariance, and the maps were written into a DeepGIS and Folium stack so posterior mean and uncertainty could be inspected as planning products.

The recurring problem was deciding which observations were usable. The sonde, the sonar and the navigation estimate each degraded differently on open water, so admission to the analysis had to be defined per sensor rather than per timestamp.
